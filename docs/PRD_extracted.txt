1. Executive Summary
Medikart is a custom Pharmacy E-Commerce and Pharmacy Management System that replaces manual,
spreadsheet-driven order handling with a real online store and an internal management dashboard — while
still feeding the client's existing Google Sheets workflow so nothing downstream breaks on day one.
The product ships as two connected experiences: a guest-checkout storefront (Next.js, for search-engine
visibility) where customers browse and order medicines, and an admin dashboard (React + Vite) where
pharmacy staff manage the catalog, process three distinct order types (Standard, Instant, and Narcotics), and
run the day-to-day business — including full control over which individual products carry the Narcotics flag.
1.1 Problem Statement
● Customers currently have no self-service way to order medicines online from this pharmacy.
● Order intake, prescription handling, and delivery-cost calculation are manual, error-prone, and hard
to scale to a real catalog of 6,112 products across 15 categories.
● Narcotics/controlled-substance orders need a verification step that a generic e-commerce checkout
does not provide out of the box.
1.2 Solution Summary
A custom React/Next.js/Node/MongoDB application (not a WordPress or theme-based build) that gives
customers a fast, search-engine-visible guest checkout, gives staff a single dashboard to run the pharmacy,
and gives the client's existing Google Sheets-based reporting a live, automatic feed — with a purpose-built
narcotics verification flow and full admin control over which products require it.
1.3 Catalog Snapshot
Based on the client's actual stock file: 6,112 real products across 15 categories (Medicines, Consumer,
Vitamins, Nutraceuticals, Dermatology, Milk & Powder, Surgical, Herbal, Fridge Items, Diagnostics, Diapers &
Napkins, Flat Items, Beverages, Patient Supports, General Items, Surgical Furniture — with "Nutra" merged
into "Nutraceuticals" as a duplicate). 506 products currently have no category assigned and will need one
before launch. Product photography is being generated by the development team using AI rather than
supplied by the client.

2. Goals & Objectives
Goal Success Looks Like
Enable self-service online ordering A customer can find a medicine, check out as a guest, and receive confirmation — in
under a few minutes.
Support prescription-based ordering A customer with a prescription but no product knowledge can still order via Instant
Order.
Enforce narcotics compliance No narcotics order is fulfilled or card-charged without an approved prescription.
Give staff full catalog control, Admins can add/remove the Narcotics flag on any product at any time, with a full
including narcotics classification audit trail — no engineering involvement required.
Preserve the client's existing reporting Every order lands correctly in the right Google Sheets tab, and a weekly Excel report
workflow is auto-emailed.
Support the client's planned Excel 6,000–8,000 existing products import cleanly with error reporting, not a silent partial
catalog import.

3. Target Users & Personas
3.1 The Guest Shopper
Orders medicines for themselves or family, wants to avoid creating an account, may pay COD or by card, and
cares most about knowing the total cost (including delivery) before committing.
3.2 The Prescription Customer
Has a doctor's prescription, doesn't necessarily know exact product names/SKUs, and wants to hand off the
prescription and let the pharmacy figure out the order (Instant Order) — sometimes for medicines that are
narcotics-controlled and require verification.
3.3 Pharmacy Staff / Admin
Processes incoming orders all day, needs to move fast through a dense dashboard, needs to review
prescriptions (including narcotics approvals/rejections), assign branches, and keep the product catalog —
including which products count as narcotics — accurate as regulations or stock changes.
3.4 The Client (Business Owner)
Wants visibility into sales, wants the existing Google Sheets-based operations to keep working, and wants
confidence that narcotics sales are compliant and auditable without needing to involve a developer every
time a product's classification changes.

4. Product Scope
4.1 In Scope
● Guest-checkout customer website: browse, search, cart, checkout, OTP verification, COD/card
payment, automatic delivery pricing.
● Three order types: Standard, Instant Order, Narcotics (verification-gated).
● Admin dashboard: Overview, Products (incl. Narcotics flagging), Categories, Orders, Cities, Branch
Assignment, Sales Reports, Admin Users, Settings, Activity Logs.
● Integrations: MongoDB Atlas, self-hosted image storage, Google Sheets (two worksheets), SMTP
email, Habib Metro payment gateway.
● Bulk product import from the client's existing Excel catalog.
● Discounts at product/category/storewide level, with a fixed precedence rule and server-side price
integrity.
● AI symptom-assistant chatbot (Groq LLM), About page, Contact Us page, and a WhatsApp click-to-
chat icon.
● Super Admin ability to create/delete admin accounts and assign their role/module access.
4.2 Out of Scope (This Phase)
● Customer accounts/login (guest checkout only, by client requirement).
● Automatic low-stock threshold tracking (manual In Stock / Out of Stock only, for now).
● Fixed named role presets beyond Super Admin/Admin (e.g. a dedicated "Sales Manager" preset) —
module-level access toggles cover this for now (FR-AD-38); named presets can be layered on later
without a data model change.
● Multi-branch inventory allocation (Branch Description is descriptive/manual in this phase).

6. Non-Functional Requirements
Non-functional requirements describe how the system must behave, not just what it must do —
performance, security, compliance, reliability, usability, and maintainability targets that apply across every
feature above.
ID Category Requirement
NFR-PERF-01 Performance Product listing and search pages shall return results within 2 seconds under normal
load (catalog of up to 8,000 products).
NFR-PERF-02 Performance The admin dashboard order list shall paginate/server-filter rather than loading the
full order history client-side.
NFR-SCAL-01 Scalability The backend API shall run under a process manager (PM2) in cluster mode to scale
across available CPU cores.
NFR-SCAL-02 Scalability The data model shall support adding new pharmacy branches without a schema
migration.
NFR-SEC-01 Security All traffic shall be served over HTTPS with a valid SSL certificate (Let's Encrypt), auto-
renewed, with HSTS enabled so browsers refuse to downgrade to HTTP.
NFR-SEC-02 Security Admin passwords shall be stored using a salted one-way hash (bcrypt); plaintext
passwords shall never be logged, stored, or emailed — including during password
reset (FR-AD-32).
NFR-SEC-03 Security OTP codes and password-reset tokens shall expire quickly (OTP: 10 minutes; reset
link: 30 minutes), be single-use, and be rate-limited per email/IP to prevent brute
force.
NFR-SEC-04 Security Prescription and product images shall be validated for true file type (not extension
alone) and size before upload is accepted.
NFR-SEC-05 Security All admin-only routes shall require authentication by default (allow-list, not block-list,
for public endpoints).
NFR-SEC-06 Security Secrets (DB URI, SMTP credentials, Habib Metro gateway keys, JWT signing secret)
shall live only in an untracked .env file per environment, never committed to source
control.
NFR-SEC-07 Security Only a Super Admin account shall be able to create, delete, or edit another admin
account's role/access; a regular Admin account shall not be able to escalate its own
privileges.
NFR-SEC-08 Security Admin accounts shall support two-factor authentication (2FA), given the dashboard
approves narcotics orders and handles payment references.
NFR-SEC-09 Security Session cookies shall be HttpOnly, Secure, and SameSite; admin state-changing
requests shall be protected against CSRF.
NFR-SEC-10 Security The application shall set standard security response headers (via helmet.js or
equivalent) covering clickjacking, MIME-sniffing, and a Content-Security-Policy.
NFR-SEC-11 Security The VPS shall restrict inbound network access to required ports only (firewall), use
key-based SSH authentication with password login disabled, and auto-ban IPs after
repeated failed SSH attempts (fail2ban).
NFR-SEC-12 Security Dependencies shall be scanned for known vulnerabilities (npm audit / Dependabot)
on an ongoing basis, not only at initial build.
NFR-SEC-13 Security A security review (at minimum an automated scan such as OWASP ZAP against
staging) shall be completed before the site accepts real customer payments.

9. Discounts & Promotions
Client requirement: discounts can be applied at three levels — a single product, a whole category, or the
entire catalog — and removed just as easily, with a percentage of the admin's choosing (e.g. 4%, 5%, 6%).
9.1 Precedence Rule
Note: Confirmed with the client: the most specific discount wins. A product-level discount overrides a
category-level discount, which overrides a storewide discount. Discounts never stack.
● Product-level: set on an individual product's edit screen — takes priority over everything else for that
product.
● Category-level: set once on a category — applies to every product inside it that doesn't have its own
product-level discount.
● Storewide: one setting in Settings — applies to any product that has neither a product-level nor a
category-level discount active.
● Removing a discount at any level simply falls back to the next-broadest one still active, or full price if
none remain — "remove" and "apply 0%" are the same action, not a separate feature.
9.2 Display & Pricing Integrity
On the storefront, a discounted product shows its original price struck through next to the new price, plus a
percentage-off badge — for example:
PKR 3,850 PKR 2,200 43% OFF
Note: The effective (discounted) price is always calculated server-side, at the moment of display and again
at the moment of checkout — never trusted from the customer's browser. This is the same principle
already applied to the delivery-charge calculation (FR-CW-11); a price is not something the client is ever
allowed to assert on its own.

17. Security Protocols
This section consolidates every security measure across the system into one reference, structured around the
OWASP API Security Top 10 (2023) — the industry-standard checklist for exactly the kind of REST API this
project is built on — plus the transport, infrastructure, and payment-specific protocols already established
elsewhere in this document. Nothing here is new policy; it is the existing NFR-SEC requirements (Section 6)
and Section 16 organized as a single security reference, with the rate-limiting approach specifically modeled
on how production API providers (Stripe, GitHub, Cloudflare) actually run theirs, not just OWASP's general
guidance.
17.1 Transport & Network Security
● HTTPS enforced everywhere (Let's Encrypt SSL), with HTTP requests redirected and HSTS set so a
browser refuses to downgrade even if asked to.
● Cloudflare in front of the VPS: DDoS absorption, a basic Web Application Firewall, and edge caching
— the same pattern used by most production APIs to keep abusive traffic from ever reaching the
origin server.
● VPS firewall restricts inbound access to required ports only; SSH is key-only (no password login), with
fail2ban auto-banning IPs after repeated failed attempts.
17.2 API Rate Limiting — Every Endpoint, Not Just Login/OTP
Note: Direct answer: yes, this is included. Every API route is rate-limited, not only the sensitive ones — the
difference is how strict the limit is per route, not whether one exists.
The approach follows the same pattern used by Stripe and GitHub in production: a Redis-backed token-
bucket limiter (Stripe's own engineering write-ups describe exactly this — a per-account bucket in Redis, a
token removed per request, refilled at a steady rate), which tolerates normal bursts of legitimate traffic while
still capping abuse. Key design points:
● Global baseline limit applied to every endpoint by default — nothing is unlimited or forgotten (this is
precisely OWASP's API4: Unrestricted Resource Consumption, the modern name for what used to be
called "lack of rate limiting").
● Tighter, endpoint-specific overrides on sensitive routes: OTP requests, login attempts, password
reset, and the chatbot endpoint get materially stricter limits than a public product listing call —
mirroring how Stripe applies much lower per-endpoint limits on top of its global cap for specific
sensitive operations.
● Rate limiting keys on a composite of IP + account/session, not IP alone — protects against both a
single attacker and the case of many legitimate users sharing one IP (office network, mobile carrier
NAT), a known failure mode called out in current API security guidance.
● A client that exceeds its limit receives HTTP 429 with a Retry-After header — a clear, standard signal,
not a silent failure or an unrelated error.
● Counters live in Redis specifically so limits are enforced correctly across all PM2 cluster processes —
a per-process in-memory counter would let an attacker bypass the limit just by hitting a different
process.
17.3 Authentication & Session Security
● Admin passwords hashed with bcrypt; plaintext never logged, stored, or emailed at any point,
including password reset.

● Two-factor authentication available on admin accounts, given the dashboard approves narcotics
orders and handles payment references.
● OTP and password-reset tokens are short-lived and single-use (OWASP API2: Broken Authentication

● Session cookies are HttpOnly, Secure, and SameSite; admin state-changing requests carry CSRF

17.4 Authorization — Object, Property, and Function Level
Three distinct OWASP API risks are addressed explicitly, since they are the most common real-world API
breach pattern and easy to miss if authorization is only checked "is this user logged in" rather than "is this
user allowed to touch this specific thing":
● Object-level (API1 — BOLA): every endpoint that returns or modifies a specific record (an order, an
admin account) verifies the requester actually owns or is authorized for that exact object — not just
that they're authenticated in general. This is what stops someone from changing an order ID in a
request and pulling up a stranger's order.
● Property-level / mass assignment (API3 — BOPLA): every write endpoint accepts an explicit allow-list
of fields for that request type. A checkout request cannot set isNarcotic or price, no matter what's
included in the payload — those fields simply aren't recognized outside the admin product-edit
endpoint that's supposed to touch them.
● Function-level (API5 — BFLA): Super-Admin-only actions (creating/editing other admins, per FR-AD-
37/38) are enforced in the backend on every request, not just hidden from the menu in the UI — a
regular Admin calling that API route directly is still rejected.
17.5 Input Validation & Injection Prevention
● Every request body is validated against a schema (zod/Joi) before it reaches any business logic —
malformed or unexpected input is rejected at the door, not handled defensively deeper in the code.
● MongoDB access goes through Mongoose's parameterized queries exclusively — no hand-built query
strings from user input, which is what prevents NoSQL injection.
● Maximum payload sizes are enforced (request body size, array lengths, upload size) — an
unbounded payload is itself a resource-consumption attack per OWASP API4.
17.6 Web Application Protections
● Standard security headers via helmet.js: clickjacking protection, MIME-sniffing prevention, and a
Content-Security-Policy.
● Output is escaped by default (React/Next.js); anywhere raw HTML must render (e.g. rich product
descriptions), it is explicitly sanitized rather than trusted.
17.7 File Upload Security
● Every upload (product image, prescription) is validated by real file content/type, not by trusting the
filename extension, and capped at a maximum size.
● Prescription uploads are stored outside any public route, reachable only through an authenticated
admin endpoint — never a guessable public URL.

19. UI Requirements
19.1 Direction
Full design tokens and rationale live in the companion Design Direction document. In summary: a calm,
trustworthy "modernized apothecary" look — deep green and a single warm amber accent rather than a
generic clinical blue — with a recurring "label-stub" motif used to surface status (In Stock, Pending
Verification, Instant Order, Narcotics) at a glance across both the storefront and the dashboard.
19.2 Requirements
● Fully responsive across mobile, tablet, and desktop for the customer website.
● Dense, sortable/filterable data tables for the admin dashboard, optimized for high order volume,
with a product search-by-name/SKU field always visible (FR-AD-31).
● Narcotics-flagged products are shown with a badge/flyer on the product card — never as a separate
navigable category — and pending-verification orders must be visually distinguishable at a glance,
without being alarming (neutral card, red label-stub only).
● Placeholder imagery for any product without a photo — never a broken image.
● Motion used sparingly and purposefully; respects prefers-reduced-motion.
● Storefront pages are server-rendered (Next.js) with per-page titles/descriptions and Open Graph tags
— see Section 19.3 for the full SEO plan this enables.
19.3 SEO & Discoverability
This is the direct reason the storefront is built in Next.js rather than a plain React SPA — none of the below is
reliably achievable without server-rendered HTML.
● Auto-generated sitemap.xml (from the live product/category data) and robots.txt, submitted
through Google Search Console.
● JSON-LD structured data: Product schema (price, stock, rating) on every product page, Organization
and LocalBusiness schema site-wide — LocalBusiness matters most here, since it's what makes
Medikart eligible to appear in local/map-pack results for Pakistani searches.
● Google Business Profile listing(s) for each physical branch, verified and kept current (hours, phone) —
for a pharmacy, this typically drives as much visibility as the website itself.
Note: Technical SEO (this section) can be fully in place at launch. Actual ranking movement into the top 10–
25 for competitive search terms realistically takes 3–6 months of ongoing content, local reviews, and
backlink work on top of this foundation — worth setting that expectation with the client explicitly, so
launch day isn't mistaken for the finish line.

24. User Journeys
24.1 Guest Shopper — Standard Order
Amina needs paracetamol and a cough syrup tonight. She opens Medikart on her phone, searches
"paracetamol," adds it and a cough syrup to her cart, and goes to checkout. She enters her name, phone, and
address, gets an OTP by email, verifies it, chooses Cash on Delivery, sees the delivery charge for her city (PKR
250, since her city is configured), and places the order. She gets one confirmation email and waits for delivery
— no account, no password to remember.
24.2 Prescription Customer — Instant Order
Bilal has a prescription from his doctor but doesn't know the exact brand names his pharmacist would
normally stock. Instead of guessing through search, he selects Instant Order, photographs and uploads the
prescription, fills in his details, verifies by OTP, and submits. A pharmacist reviews the prescription, adds the
correct medicines and a total, and the order proceeds — Bilal never had to identify a single SKU himself.
24.3 Narcotics Customer
Hina's prescription includes a controlled medication the pharmacy has flagged as Narcotics. When she adds it
to her cart, checkout requires her to upload the prescription before she can complete the order. Her order
goes to Pending Verification; a pharmacist reviews and approves it, and only then — if she chose card
payment — is her card actually charged. If the prescription had been invalid, she would never have been
charged at all.
24.4 Pharmacy Admin — Narcotics Reclassification
A regulation update means a product the pharmacy previously sold freely now needs prescription control.
The admin opens that product, flags it as Narcotics, and from that moment every new order for it is routed
into verification — with no wait for a developer, no deployment, and a logged record of exactly who made
the change and when, in case it's ever questioned.
